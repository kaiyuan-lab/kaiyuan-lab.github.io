RESEARCH

From genome sequence to cellular possibility.

Cells with essentially the same genome can adopt remarkably different identities. We seek to understand how the genome defines the range of cellular states a cell can access, how this potential is organized during development, and how it is reshaped in disease.

OUR QUESTION

Cell fate asks which state is chosen.
We ask which states remain possible.

Gene regulatory networks are central to determining which fate a cell adopts. We are interested in a complementary question: what determines the range of states available to a cell in the first place? We propose that this cellular possibility emerges from multiple layers of genome organization. DNA sequence, chromatin architecture, and nuclear organization together create a structured landscape in which some cellular states are accessible while others are constrained.

AN EVOLUTIONARY VIEW OF THE GENOME

Genomes are evolving ecosystems.

Genomes are not static repositories of genes. They are products of evolutionary cooperation, competition, invasion, and conflict. Gene-centered views of evolution and the concept of selfish genetic elements, articulated in works such as The Selfish Gene (Dawkins, 1976) and Genes in Conflict (Burt & Trivers, 2006), provide a powerful perspective on why repetitive and mobile sequences occupy such a substantial fraction of eukaryotic genomes.

Yet conflict is only part of the story. Host genomes evolve mechanisms to recognize and constrain repetitive elements, while repeat-derived sequences and functions can also be co-opted into cellular regulatory systems. This continuing interplay can generate Red Queen-like cycles of genomic innovation and host adaptation.

Repetitive elements are therefore more than genomic passengers. Their evolutionary histories have shaped both genome sequence and the molecular systems that recognize it. Modern studies further suggest that repeat-derived sequences contribute not only to gene regulation but also to higher-order genome architecture (Lawson et al., 2023).

This leads to a possibility that particularly interests us: evolution may have embedded principles of genome organization within the repetitive genome itself. If genome organization helps determine which cellular states remain accessible, understanding how that organization is encoded, regulated, and remodeled may reveal how the genome defines cellular possibility.

THREE LAYERS OF OUR RESEARCH
01
ENCODE

How does the genome encode nuclear organization?

A large fraction of the eukaryotic genome consists of repetitive sequences. Rather than viewing these sequences simply as genomic excess or elements that must be silenced, we ask whether they contain information that contributes to the physical organization of the genome.

Our work investigates how repetitive sequences acquire distinct chromatin states and how repeat-specific regulatory mechanisms contribute to higher-order nuclear organization. We are particularly interested in how these organizational properties emerge during development and how they diversify through evolution.

Genome sequence may encode not only genetic information, but also principles of genome organization.

02
REGULATE

How does nuclear organization regulate cellular possibility?

Genome organization does not stop at chromatin. Proteins, nucleic acids, chromatin domains, and nuclear bodies assemble dynamically in three-dimensional space, creating biochemical environments that concentrate some molecular interactions while excluding others. Intrinsically disordered protein regions provide a particularly versatile platform for such organization because their conformational behavior and interactions can be tuned by sequence and chemical context (Holehouse & Kragelund, 2024). Biomolecular condensates provide a higher-order manifestation of these multivalent interactions and have emerged as an important organizational layer in transcription (Pei et al., 2025).

We investigate how these higher-order structures are regulated and how they influence cell-state transitions. A major focus is O-GlcNAcylation, a metabolism-sensitive post-translational modification enriched on many nuclear and intrinsically disordered proteins. We ask how metabolic information is translated into changes in chromatin organization, nuclear condensates, and developmental potential.

Cellular possibility is not encoded by sequence alone; it emerges from genome organization in space and time.

03
REWIRE

How does disease rewire cellular possibility?

The same organizational systems that stabilize cellular identity can become reconfigured in disease. Cancer is a particularly striking example: cells can escape established lineage constraints, reactivate normally inaccessible genomic programs, and acquire states that support progression and therapeutic resistance.

We study how cancer cells exploit the repetitive genome to achieve this plasticity. Endogenous retroviruses are especially intriguing because they preserve regulatory sequences, RNAs, and in some cases protein-coding potential inherited from ancient viral infections. Their activity has also been linked to developmental cell-state potential (Macfarlan et al., 2012).

Rather than treating endogenous retrovirus activation simply as a consequence of epigenetic instability, we ask when repeat-derived activities become functional components of tumor regulatory networks—and whether these dependencies create therapeutic vulnerabilities.

Disease can rewire genome organization to unlock cellular possibilities that are normally constrained.
